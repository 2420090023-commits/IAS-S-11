The history of cryptography stretches back thousands of years, intertwining with
the evolution of human communication and the perpetual need for secrecy. From the
earliest substitution ciphers used by the ancient Egyptians to the sophisticated
algorithms that protect modern digital infrastructure, the art and science of
encoding information has played a pivotal role in shaping civilizations, winning
wars, and safeguarding personal privacy.

In ancient Rome, Julius Caesar employed a simple shift cipher — now known as the
Caesar Cipher — to communicate with his generals on the battlefield. Each letter
in the plaintext was replaced by a letter a fixed number of positions down the
alphabet. Although trivially breakable by modern standards, this technique was
remarkably effective in an era when literacy itself was rare. The Caesar Cipher
represents one of the earliest known examples of a substitution cipher, and it
laid the groundwork for centuries of cryptographic development.

During the Renaissance, polyalphabetic ciphers emerged as a significant advance
over their monoalphabetic predecessors. The Vigenere Cipher, attributed to the
French diplomat Blaise de Vigenere, used a repeating keyword to shift each letter
of the plaintext by a variable amount. This made frequency analysis — the primary
tool for breaking substitution ciphers — significantly more difficult. For nearly
three centuries, the Vigenere Cipher was considered unbreakable and earned the
nickname "le chiffre indechiffrable" (the indecipherable cipher).

The advent of mechanical encryption devices in the early twentieth century marked
another quantum leap. The Enigma machine, developed by the German engineer Arthur
Scherbius, used a series of rotating electromechanical rotors to scramble
plaintext messages into seemingly random ciphertext. During World War II, the
German military relied heavily on the Enigma machine for secure communications.
The Allied effort to crack the Enigma, spearheaded by the brilliant mathematician
Alan Turing and his team at Bletchley Park, is widely credited with shortening
the war by an estimated two years and saving countless lives.

The birth of modern cryptography is often traced to Claude Shannon's landmark
1949 paper, "Communication Theory of Secrecy Systems," which laid the
mathematical foundations for provably secure encryption. Shannon demonstrated
that the one-time pad — a cipher in which the key is as long as the message and
used only once — provides perfect secrecy, meaning that the ciphertext reveals
absolutely no information about the plaintext. While impractical for most real-
world applications due to the difficulty of securely distributing and managing
such long keys, the one-time pad remains theoretically important as the gold
standard of cryptographic security.

The 1970s witnessed two revolutionary developments. First, the Data Encryption
Standard (DES) was adopted by the U.S. National Bureau of Standards (now NIST) as
a federal standard for encrypting sensitive but unclassified information. DES is a
symmetric-key block cipher that operates on 64-bit blocks of data using a 56-bit
key. It employs a Feistel network structure with 16 rounds of substitution and
permutation operations. Although DES served admirably for over two decades, its
56-bit key length eventually became vulnerable to brute-force attacks as computing
power increased exponentially.

Second, and perhaps more importantly, Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman
published their groundbreaking paper "New Directions in Cryptography" in 1976,
introducing the concept of public-key cryptography. This paradigm-shifting idea
allowed two parties to establish a shared secret key over an insecure channel
without having previously exchanged any secret information. The Diffie-Hellman
key exchange protocol, based on the mathematical difficulty of computing discrete
logarithms in finite fields, remains widely used today as a fundamental building
block of secure communication protocols.

Shortly after, Ron Rivest, Adi Shamir, and Leonard Adleman developed the RSA
algorithm, the first practical public-key cryptosystem. RSA relies on the
computational difficulty of factoring the product of two large prime numbers.
The algorithm enables both encryption and digital signatures, making it one of
the most versatile and widely deployed cryptographic algorithms in history. RSA
key sizes have grown over the years — from 512 bits in the early days to 2048 or
4096 bits today — to keep pace with advances in factoring algorithms and
computational power.

As DES reached the end of its useful life, the search for a replacement led to
the Advanced Encryption Standard (AES) competition, organized by NIST in 1997.
After a rigorous multi-year evaluation process involving fifteen candidate
algorithms from around the world, the Rijndael cipher — designed by the Belgian
cryptographers Joan Daemen and Vincent Rijmen — was selected as the new standard
in 2001. AES supports key sizes of 128, 192, and 256 bits, and operates on 128-
bit blocks of data. It uses a substitution-permutation network (SPN) architecture
with 10, 12, or 14 rounds depending on the key size.

AES has become the de facto global standard for symmetric encryption. It is used
to protect classified U.S. government information, secure financial transactions,
encrypt internet traffic via TLS and HTTPS, and safeguard data at rest in storage
systems worldwide. The algorithm's elegant mathematical structure, combined with
its resistance to all known cryptanalytic attacks and its efficiency in both
software and hardware implementations, has earned it the trust of governments,
corporations, and individuals alike.

The AES algorithm operates through a series of well-defined transformations
applied to a 4x4 matrix of bytes called the State. Each round consists of four
main operations: SubBytes (a non-linear byte substitution using a fixed lookup
table called the S-box), ShiftRows (a cyclic shift of each row by a different
offset), MixColumns (a linear mixing operation on the columns using polynomial
multiplication over a Galois field), and AddRoundKey (an XOR operation with a
portion of the expanded key). The final round omits the MixColumns step.

The key expansion algorithm, also known as the Rijndael key schedule, derives a
series of round keys from the original cipher key. For AES-256, the original
256-bit key is expanded into fifteen 128-bit round keys (one for the initial
AddRoundKey step plus one for each of the fourteen rounds). The key schedule uses
a combination of byte substitution, rotation, and XOR operations with round
constants to ensure that each round key is cryptographically distinct from the
original key and from every other round key.

Modern cryptographic practice employs AES in various modes of operation to handle
data of arbitrary length. The most common modes include Electronic Codebook (ECB),
which encrypts each block independently (not recommended for most applications
due to pattern leakage); Cipher Block Chaining (CBC), which XORs each plaintext
block with the previous ciphertext block before encryption; Counter Mode (CTR),
which converts the block cipher into a stream cipher by encrypting successive
counter values; and Galois Counter Mode (GCM), which provides both authenticated
encryption and data integrity verification.

In this laboratory exercise, we implement AES-256 in CBC mode using the
PyCryptodome library, a self-contained Python package of low-level cryptographic
primitives. PyCryptodome provides production-quality implementations of AES and
other algorithms, rigorously tested against official test vectors and optimized
for performance. By using PyCryptodome, we can focus on understanding the
protocol-level aspects of secure communication — key management, initialization
vector generation, padding, and network transmission — while relying on a
trusted library for the core cryptographic operations.

The client-server architecture demonstrated in this task mirrors the fundamental
structure of real-world secure communication systems. The client generates a
random AES-256 key, encrypts a plaintext message, and transmits the ciphertext
along with the initialization vector to the server. The server uses the shared
key to decrypt the message, verifying that the decrypted plaintext matches the
original. The same process is then reversed, with the server encrypting its own
message and sending it back to the client for decryption.

This bidirectional encrypted file transfer demonstrates several important
security principles: confidentiality (the plaintext is never transmitted in the
clear), key management (the AES key must be securely shared between the
communicating parties), initialization vector uniqueness (a fresh random IV is
generated for each encryption operation to ensure semantic security), and data
integrity (any modification to the ciphertext during transmission will result in
garbled plaintext upon decryption, alerting the recipient to potential tampering).

As we continue to advance into an era of quantum computing, the field of
cryptography faces new challenges and opportunities. Quantum computers, once
sufficiently powerful, could theoretically break many of the public-key
algorithms currently in use, including RSA and elliptic curve cryptography.
However, symmetric algorithms like AES are expected to remain secure against
quantum attacks, provided that key sizes are doubled (e.g., AES-256 instead of
AES-128) to account for Grover's algorithm, which effectively halves the
security level of symmetric ciphers.

The ongoing development of post-quantum cryptographic algorithms, standardized
through NIST's Post-Quantum Cryptography initiative, represents the next
frontier in the eternal battle between codemakers and codebreakers. Lattice-based

(for digital signatures) are expected to provide security against both classical
and quantum adversaries. These new algorithms will eventually complement or
